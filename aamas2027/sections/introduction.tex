\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

% DOCX paragraph 006.
From climate change and critical minerals to energy resources and disaster risk, Earth science seeks to turn observations into testable scientific judgments. These research frontiers share data-intensive, computation-intensive, and interdisciplinary characteristics, and geoscientific research is moving toward predictive systems science driven jointly by big data and artificial intelligence~\cite{zheng2025frontiers}. In this emerging paradigm, scientific progress depends not only on the scale of data and models, but also on the ability to organize distributed data, tools, and methods into reproducible analytical workflows.

% DOCX paragraph 007. The empty citation in the source is filled here.
Traditional geoscientific analysis relies heavily on researchers manually connecting data-processing stages. Data are distributed across databases, publications, and repositories, while differences in reporting standards impede comparison and integration~\cite{zheng2025frontiers}. Researchers typically locate data, align them spatially or temporally, derive variables, perform statistical or model-based computations, and verify the results. Resource and disaster analyses may additionally combine retrieval, spatial processing, and interdisciplinary models. This process is labor-intensive and difficult to scale. Scientific agents built around large language models (LLMs) offer an opportunity to automate such workflows: they translate natural-language tasks into multistep plans, coordinate multiple tools, and have shown promise in scientific analysis~\cite{ding2025scitoolagent,feng2026earthagent}. However, geoscientific workflows have a defining feature that distinguishes them from ordinary question answering: later operations depend on artifacts produced by earlier steps. Model memory or a single tool call is insufficient to complete the entire processing chain. For scientific agents to support geoscientific research, they must reliably orchestrate multistep tool chains and produce conclusions grounded in their execution.

% DOCX paragraph 009.
Building agents with tool-orchestration capabilities first requires evaluations that can test those capabilities. Benchmarking has consequently evolved from testing knowledge to examining processes. Early work centered on knowledge questions and multiround scientific conversations. EarthSE, for example, evaluates geoscientific reasoning through question answering and exploratory dialogue~\cite{xu2025earthse}, but does not require actual tool execution and therefore cannot evaluate the analytical process. GeoBenchX~\cite{krechetova2025geobenchx}, Earth-Bench~\cite{feng2026earthagent}, and GeoAgentBench~\cite{yu2026geoagentbench} explicitly examine multistep tool use; the latter two also evaluate processes and final outcomes. Nevertheless, a gap remains: existing benchmarks do not comprehensively cover a broad range of geoscientific research themes while jointly testing composition and orchestration across tools. This limits their ability to distinguish answering a geoscientific question from completing its analytical workflow within the same task, and to locate the stage at which orchestration fails. Such attribution is needed to guide methodological improvement.

% DOCX paragraph 012.
At the methodological level, ReAct~\cite{yao2023react}, SciToolAgent~\cite{ding2025scitoolagent}, and Voyager~\cite{wang2023voyager} explore agent planning and execution through interaction feedback, tool knowledge graphs, and skill reuse, respectively. Relative to the requirements above, however, existing approaches do not jointly resolve operation-level reuse and constraint preservation. Planning directly around tool names couples plans to interface changes, while reusing an entire Skill limits access to local operations from different task strategies. An open problem is therefore how to select and combine the operations needed for a task from different strategies, preserve their original prerequisites, and choose suitable concrete tools for their implementation.

% DOCX paragraphs 013--017. The duplicated contribution lead-in is merged.
To address these two challenges, we introduce GeoToolEval, a benchmark for tool-workflow orchestration, and AgentGS, an orchestration method based on hierarchical capability composition. GeoToolEval provides an evaluation setting for complete geoscientific tool chains, while AgentGS provides a method for constructing and executing those chains. Our contributions are as follows:
\begin{itemize}
  \item We construct GeoToolEval, comprehensively covering ten geoscientific research-frontier themes with 603 evaluation records. Its combination of single-step and multistep tool-use tasks jointly examines tool dependencies, composition across tools, and final answers, providing signals that support attribution of orchestration failures.
  \item We propose AgentGS, a geoscientific tool-orchestration agent based on a Skill--Capability--Tool hierarchy and hypergraph-based missing-capability completion. It supports reuse across strategies and constrained tool selection, and fills missing operations to improve tool use and task completion.
  \item On all 603 GeoToolEval records, AgentGS achieves a mean FAS of 0.8731 and substantially outperforms the evaluated geoscientific language models and agents. Analyses of multistep tasks show that the proposed strategies supply necessary operations and improve completion and answer accuracy for complex tasks.
\end{itemize}
